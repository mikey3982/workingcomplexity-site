These are the system.

The Working Complexity manifesto is not a positioning statement. It is a diagnosis — and a refusal to look away from what the healthcare system keeps calling an exception.

Working Complexity
16 May 2026  ·  5 min read

Healthcare systems are designed, in the main, around the typical. The average patient. The expected presentation. The pathway that flows. What sits outside that average — the person who does not speak English, the family holding three conflicting diagnoses, the clinician who is not certain and knows it — is handled as deviation. As exception. As overhead.

We think that is wrong. Not just ethically — though it is that — but analytically. The edge is not a rounding error. It is information. The places where a system struggles to accommodate a person are precisely the places where the system's assumptions are most clearly visible.

The patient is not an edge case.
The family is not overhead.
The clinician's uncertainty is not a failure.
These are the system.

This is the Working Complexity manifesto. Four sentences. We have found that it either lands immediately or requires some unpacking — and the unpacking is usually the more useful conversation.

What it means in practice

The patient is not an edge case.

Every patient is, in some sense, an edge case. Their history, their comorbidities, their living situation, their relationship with the system — these are specific, irreducible, and mostly invisible to the pathway that receives them. Designing as if patients are interchangeable is not a simplification. It is a category error.

When we map a care pathway, we map it as it is actually experienced — not as the flowchart describes it. We talk to the person who uses the wheelchair that the corridor was not designed for. We talk to the woman who attends every appointment alone because there is no interpreter for her language. We hold that specificity carefully. It is not anecdote. It is the system, showing us itself.

Not a deviation from the design. Evidence of what the design missed.

The family is not overhead.

The family member in the waiting room. The daughter translating a discharge letter. The husband who knows — because he lives with her — that the medication schedule will not work. These people are not an administrative inconvenience. They are doing clinical work, unpaid, unrecognised, and often unsupported.

Healthcare systems that treat informal carers as peripheral do so because accounting for them is complicated. We think complication is not a reason to look away. It is a reason to look harder.

The clinician's uncertainty is not a failure.

Medicine is, at its edges, uncertain. The diagnosis that does not quite fit. The treatment response that is not what the literature predicted. The patient who presents twice in a week and the consultant who does not yet know why. Uncertainty is not a gap in competence. It is honest contact with complexity.

Systems that punish uncertainty — through audit frameworks, through liability culture, through the expectation of confident documentation — do not eliminate it. They drive it underground. The result is less information, not more.

How we work

The manifesto is not aspirational. It describes the lens through which we enter every engagement. We work across three dimensions — not sequentially, but together.

  • 01 / 03 Understand

    Deep thinking, sense-making, systems and complexity theory. We spend time in the situation before we propose anything.

  • 02 / 03 Deliver

    Prototype, pilot, make it work in the messy reality of care. Low fidelity where appropriate. High fidelity where it matters.

  • 03 / 03 Feel

    Empathy, lived experience, human depth. The work is held by people who know what it is to be on the receiving end of care.

The healthcare system does not need to be simpler. It needs to be better understood. That is the thesis. The manifesto is how we know when we are doing it honestly.

These are not edge cases. These are the system.
