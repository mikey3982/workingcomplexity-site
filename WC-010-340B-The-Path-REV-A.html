It cuts what the product earns. It also lays the path the patient walks down.

The 340B Drug Pricing Program in the United States — how the discount works, where the surplus goes, what the evidence will and will not support, and what the changes arriving on 1 January 2027 do to the care around the medicine.

§00 · The argument

A discount on the product. A subsidy for the pathway. Nobody measures the second.

Inside a pharmaceutical company, 340B appears on one line — gross-to-net — and it reads as loss. Inside a safety-net hospital or a community health centre, the same dollars appear as the pharmacist who chases the prior authorisation, the infusion chair in a rural county, the sliding-fee prescription. Both readings are true. This paper holds them together, because a board that sees only the first will misjudge what happens to its patients when the second is cut.

  1. 340B is no longer a side programme. Covered entities bought about $100 billion of outpatient medicines at 340B prices in 2025, up from $81.4 billion in 2024 and $66.3 billion in 2023. Disproportionate share hospitals account for roughly four dollars in every five.2,3
  2. The surplus is real, large and concentrated. Minnesota — the only state with published figures — found that of every $100 paid for a 340B medicine in 2024, about $50 bought the drug, $5 ran the programme and $44 stayed with the covered entity. Twenty-three large hospitals held over 80% of it.6
  3. The path is best evidenced where the money is least. Federal grantees — health centres, HIV clinics — are bound to put the income back into care, and the peer-reviewed evidence says they do. For non-profit hospitals, where most of the money sits, the evidence is mixed and the use of the surplus is unreported by design.9,10,11
  4. So our thesis is conditional, and we say so. The discount does make part of the path the patient walks down. It does not do so everywhere, and no one — not the regulator, not the manufacturer, often not the hospital — can show how much.
  5. Four instruments converge on 1 January 2027. A second round of Medicare negotiated prices, a proposed 37% cut in Medicare payment for 340B drugs, a relaunched rebate pilot, and competing reform bills. Each one shrinks or reroutes the spread. None of them says what replaces the care the spread was paying for.15,16,17,19
  6. For a manufacturer, the useful question changes. Not "how do we reduce 340B exposure" but "which parts of our patients' pathway are standing on it, and what holds them up when it moves". The ask that follows is traceability — not abolition.
  7. It belongs in our trends analysis, and it was missing. WC-006 argued that value is leaving the molecule and settling in the machinery around it. In the United States, 340B is the one place where that transfer is written into statute. We set out what it adds in §06.

Part 01 / 07
§01

How the discount works

One statutory price, two parties who see it differently, and a spread between acquisition and reimbursement that the law creates and does not direct.

1.1 · The statute

The law sets the price a manufacturer may charge. It is silent on what the buyer does next.

Section 340B of the Public Health Service Act was created by the Veterans Health Care Act of 1992.1 A manufacturer that wants its medicines covered by Medicaid and Medicare Part B must offer them to defined "covered entities" at or below a ceiling price. That price is derived from the Medicaid rebate formula: the average manufacturer price less the Medicaid unit rebate — a minimum of 23.1% for most branded medicines, plus a penalty when a price has risen faster than inflation. For a product with a long history of price increases the penalty can take the ceiling price to a cent. Typical discounts have been estimated at 25–50% of the wholesale price.14

Covered entities fall into two families, and the distinction matters for everything that follows.

01 / 02 · Federal grantees
Health centres and disease-specific clinics

Federally qualified health centres, Ryan White HIV/AIDS clinics, tribal and urban Indian organisations, and other specialised clinics. They qualify through a federal grant, and the grant's own rules govern how programme income is spent.

About 13% of 340B purchases; health centres about 5%
02 / 02 · Hospitals
Disproportionate share, critical access, children's and cancer hospitals

They qualify through the population they serve — for most, a threshold share of low-income inpatients. Once qualified, the discount applies to eligible outpatients whatever their insurance. No rule attaches to the income.

About 87% of 340B purchases; DSH alone about 78%

The covered entity buys at the ceiling price. It dispenses or administers the medicine to its patient. The patient's insurer — commercial plan, Medicare, sometimes Medicaid — reimburses at its ordinary rate. The difference stays with the covered entity. Congress intended this: the purpose recorded in 1992 was to let these providers stretch scarce federal resources further. What the statute does not do is require that the discount reach the patient at the counter, or that the entity report where the difference goes.2

1.2 · How it grew

The programme did not grow because more patients became poor. It grew because more prescriptions became eligible.

The Congressional Budget Office traced spending through the prime vendor from $6.6 billion in 2010 to $43.9 billion in 2021 — 19% a year, against 4% for branded medicines across the market. About a third of that growth followed the market: more spending on cancer and anti-infective medicines, where 340B providers are concentrated. The other two thirds came from three structural changes.4

  • Hospitals integrated with outpatient clinics. An acquired oncology or specialty practice can be registered as a "child site", and its prescriptions become 340B-eligible. CBO judged this the largest single contributor. Registered sites rose from under 30,000 in 2014 to over 60,000 by February 2025.2,4
  • The Affordable Care Act widened eligibility to critical access hospitals, rural referral centres and others.
  • Contract pharmacies multiplied. Guidance in 2010 allowed a covered entity to contract with any number of outside pharmacies. The count went from about 1,300 to around 20,000 by 2017, and stood at some 32,000 locations in mid-2025 — close to 60% of pharmacy locations in the country.5

CBO's conclusion is the one a board should keep in view. The programme, it found, encourages the prescribing of more and higher-priced medicines, the expansion of services, and the integration of hospitals with off-site clinics — behaviours that tend to raise federal spending. It added that the evidence is limited and the size of each effect unknown.4 Note the middle item. The expansion of services is listed as a cost. From the patient's side of the counter, it is the path.

Part 02 / 07
§02

Where the surplus goes

One state has counted the money. Nobody has counted the programmes. What can be said depends on which kind of covered entity is holding it.

2.1 · The only published ledger

Minnesota asked every covered entity what it paid, what it was paid, and what it cost to run.

Because the 340B price is confidential and federal law requires no revenue reporting, almost every national figure on the surplus is an estimate. Minnesota's legislature mandated reporting, and its Department of Health has now published two years. The 2024 data — the first to include medicines administered in clinics as well as those dispensed by pharmacies — cover 181 entities.6

Three findings carry beyond Minnesota.

The surplus is concentrated. Twenty-three general acute care hospitals — 12% of reporting entities — held $1.08 billion, over 80% of the total, at an average of $47 million each. Sixteen safety-net grantees, the health centres most people picture when they hear "340B", shared $8 million. Fifteen entities made nothing or lost money on the programme.

Commercial insurance pays for most of it. The department estimates that 45% of net 340B revenue came from commercial payers, 36% from Medicare and 19% from the state's Medicaid programmes. Whatever the surplus builds, employers and their staff are paying for nearly half of it — a cross-subsidy that appears in no premium statement. A large commercial claims study found the same shape: 340B hospitals were paid a median of 3.08 times their acquisition cost for infused medicines by commercial insurers, and kept 64% of what the insurer spent.6,8

A tenth leaks before it arrives. Covered entities paid about $137 million to contract pharmacies and third-party administrators — nearly 10% of the gross spread. For the top half of safety-net grantees, running the programme consumed at least 54% of what it generated.

What the ledger does not contain

The department is direct about the limit: the reporting "does not include information on how net 340B revenue is used nor to what extent patients are benefiting", and the data "are not audited nor otherwise verifiable".6

The most complete account of 340B money in the United States stops at the point where the question in this paper begins.

2.2 · What the surplus is said to fund

The programmes are real. The attribution is not.

Money is fungible. A hospital that receives $47 million of 340B margin and spends $47 million on a free clinic, a meds-to-beds service and an oncology navigator cannot show that one paid for the other, and is not asked to. What follows is therefore a description of what covered entities report funding, with our reading of how well each claim is supported.

Grantees — the path is a condition of the grant

A health centre must offer a sliding-fee scale; an HIV clinic's programme income is governed by its Ryan White award. A study of 1,468 federally qualified health centres from 2004 to 2022 found that more 340B sites were associated with more patients served — particularly uninsured, low-income, non-English-speaking and unhoused patients — and with more low-margin preventive services. The Senate investigation found the two health centres it examined used the money to cut patients' medicine costs.7,11

Public hospitals — services follow participation

Public hospitals that joined the programme between 2012 and 2018 were more likely to add unprofitable service lines than those that never joined.10

Non-profit hospitals — where most of the money is, and the evidence is weakest

In the same study, service provision at non-profit hospitals was largely unchanged by participation, apart from an expansion of oncology. A difference-in-differences analysis found new participation associated with a 28.9% rise in charity care spending and a wider eligibility limit for discounted care — but no change in total community benefit. A scoping review of the peer-reviewed literature concluded that the highest-quality evidence points to non-profit disproportionate share hospitals using the programme in margin-motivated ways, with inconsistent evidence of greater safety-net engagement.9,10

Hospital-reported examples are concrete, and we see no reason to doubt them individually: a Nebraska critical access hospital funding home health and transport; a Georgia safety-net system discounting outpatient medicines and opening neighbourhood clinics; a rural New York hospital sustaining chemotherapy and paying cancer patients' travel. In a 2019 member survey, 95% of hospitals said they used the savings for uncompensated care and 70% to provide free or discounted medicines.12 These are the programme's defenders describing themselves. They are also the nearest thing to a description of the path that exists.

Part 03 / 07
§03

Where the path is not built

The thesis of this paper is only worth holding if it survives the strongest version of the case against it. This is that case.

3.1 · The case against

Not a scandal. A design — one that never asked the question.

  1. The patient at the counter often pays full price. The Senate investigation found that two large health systems generated hundreds of millions of dollars in 340B revenue between 2018 and 2023 and did not pass the discounts directly to patients. Both pointed out, correctly, that Congress did not design the programme to do so.7 CMS's own survey found cases where a Medicare beneficiary's 20% co-insurance exceeded what the hospital had paid for the medicine.16
  2. The hospitals could not say what the money bought. They reported spending on "capital improvement projects" and "community benefit programs" without accounting for which expenses the revenue covered.7
  3. The charity-care comparison is unflattering, and disputed. One critical analysis put disproportionate share hospitals' 340B gain at $44.1 billion in 2022 against $18.5 billion of charity care; another found 340B hospitals spending 2.16% of operating expenses on charity care against 2.82% at others. The American Hospital Association answers that charity care is the wrong measure, and counts nearly $100 billion of total community benefit at tax-exempt 340B hospitals in the same year. The two sides are measuring different things and both have an interest in the answer.12,13
  4. Eligibility is set by the hospital's inpatients; the revenue comes from its insured outpatients. A hospital qualifies on its share of low-income admissions and earns its largest margins on commercially insured patients in outpatient oncology — who may be seen at a clinic in a prosperous suburb.
  5. It rewards the dearer medicine and the larger system. The spread is a percentage of price. CBO's finding on integration and prescribing follows from that arithmetic.4
  6. Oversight is thin. The statute bars duplicate discounts and diversion to non-patients, and lets the regulator and manufacturers audit. It does not define "patient" with any precision, and it gives the regulator little rule-making authority.2

The discount is hypothecated in the minds of its defenders and fungible in the accounts of its recipients. — Our reading of the evidence

So the honest statement of our thesis is narrower than the cover. 340B cuts what the product earns in every covered entity. It lays a path for the patient in some of them — reliably where a grant requires it, measurably in public hospitals, plausibly and unprovably in the non-profit systems that hold most of the money. A manufacturer cannot tell which of its patients are walking on that path. Neither, in most cases, can the hospital.

That is the finding. It is uncomfortable for both camps. It means the programme's critics cannot show the money is wasted, and its defenders cannot show it is not.

§04

Four instruments, one date

Negotiated prices, a Medicare payment cut, a rebate pilot and three reform bills — each designed separately, all arriving at 1 January 2027.

4.1 · The convergence

Every one of them shrinks the spread or reroutes it. None of them says what replaces what it paid for.

The Medicare payment cut has been tried before. CMS paid 340B medicines at average sales price minus 22.5% from 2018 until the Supreme Court struck the policy down in 2022 — not on its merits, but because the agency had not run the acquisition cost survey the statute requires.16 It has now run the survey.

Read these as four separate rules and each has a defensible logic: lower prices for Medicare, payment closer to cost, fewer duplicate discounts, a clearer definition. Read them as one event and something else appears. The spread is being reduced from four directions at once by people who are not required to consider — because no one has ever measured — what the spread was holding up.

The asymmetry

The outpatient payment cut is budget-neutral for hospitals as a group. It is not neutral for the hospital that runs a large specialty clinic for low-income patients. The money leaves the pharmacy line of the hospitals that dispense the most and returns as a general uplift to every hospital, in proportion to procedures.

A cross-subsidy that was at least attached to medicines-intensive care becomes one attached to nothing in particular.

Part 05 / 07
§05

Read against one disease

Interstitial lung disease as a worked example — a pathway where almost nothing that decides the outcome is paid for by the fee schedule.

5.1 · A worked example

In pulmonary fibrosis the medicine is the only part of the pathway with a price. Everything else has to be paid for from somewhere.

We use interstitial lung disease because we know its pathway well, and because it shows the mechanism cleanly. It is an illustration. We have not measured 340B's contribution to any ILD service, and we know of no one who has.

Start with what the fee schedule does not buy. A multidisciplinary discussion — pulmonologist, radiologist, pathologist, rheumatologist in one room — changes the diagnosis in a large share of cases, and no payer in the United States pays for the room.21 Nor is there a billing code in routine use for the specialty pharmacist who secures the prior authorisation, files the foundation grant when the co-payment is unaffordable, and telephones in week three when the gastrointestinal side effects begin. Antifibrotic treatment has reached about a quarter of Medicare patients with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis; in one claims cohort 43% of those who started then stopped, with average out-of-pocket costs near $400 a month.20 Cost and tolerability are both implicated. Both are managed, where they are managed at all, by people the fee schedule does not pay for.

Now place that pathway inside a covered entity. Specialist ILD care in the United States is concentrated in academic medical centres, and many of those are disproportionate share hospitals — a proportion we have not counted and which ought to be. When such a hospital dispenses an oral antifibrotic through its own specialty pharmacy to a commercially insured or Medicare patient, it buys at the 340B price and is reimbursed at the ordinary one. That margin is the most likely source of funding for the pharmacy staff who make the prescription into a treatment. It is also, in the hospital's accounts, indistinguishable from any other dollar.

Then apply §04. The leading antifibrotic was selected in the second negotiation cycle. Its negotiated price — roughly half its 2024 list price — takes effect on 1 January 2027.17 From that date the Medicare spread on the most widely used antifibrotic narrows in every covered entity at once, and its manufacturer becomes eligible for the rebate pilot. A newer agent approved in late 2025 sits years away from negotiation, with its full spread intact. Nobody designed that difference. It will nonetheless shape which medicine a hospital's finance office would prefer its ILD clinic to reach for, and which clinic can afford its pharmacist.

What we are and are not saying

We are not saying 340B funds ILD care. We are saying that the unreimbursed steps of the ILD path have to be funded from margin, that in covered entities 340B is a large part of that margin, and that the margin on the leading ILD medicine changes on a known date.

A manufacturer that has not mapped which of its prescribing centres depend on that margin will learn it from persistence data eighteen months later.

Part 06 / 07
§06

What this adds to our trends analysis

WC-006 read six health-system trends across five countries and did not mention 340B. It should have.

6.1 · The bridge

We wrote that value is leaving the molecule and settling in the machinery around it. In the United States there is a statute that does exactly that.

The central claim of WC-006 was that six apparently separate trends are one event: value moving from the medicine to the machinery that finds, classifies, entitles, reaches and retains the patient.21 We described that movement as emergent — a drift produced by payment reform, price control and commoditisation.

340B is the same movement made deliberate. It takes a defined share of a medicine's revenue and hands it to the provider, on the stated ground that the provider will use it to extend care. It is thirty-four years old, it moves tens of billions of dollars a year, and it has no equivalent in the other four systems we studied. Leaving it out made the United States look more like the others than it is.

WC-006 trend What 340B changes in the United States reading
01 · Payment stops rewarding the admission, and nothing starts rewarding the prevention We wrote that no system pays anyone to prevent the ILD admission. In covered entities, 340B margin is the nearest thing to a fund that could — unhypothecated, locally controlled, attached to outpatient medicines. It is also the fund being cut.
02 · The price is negotiated down, and the evidence bar rises to meet it The largest revision. We treated Medicare negotiation as a pricing event for the manufacturer. It is also a funding event for the provider: a lower Medicare price is a narrower spread. Price control reaches the pathway by a route we did not map.
03 · Somebody else built the detection estate Much lung-cancer screening sits in hospital outpatient departments. An incidental finding that no one is paid to act on needs a navigator; in a covered entity, that post is paid from margin.
04 · The reading bottleneck commoditises — and the discussion that matters does not "No payment system pays for the room." It remains true. But it explains how the room exists at all in some centres, and why it is fragile there.
05 · Evidence becomes continuous The rebate model requires claims-level data to flow from covered entity to manufacturer. Whatever its other effects, it creates a real-world data channel that did not exist — and a dispute about who owns it.
06 · From diagnosis to phenotype No direct change. Indirectly, the proposed "patient" definitions tie eligibility to a documented encounter within 24 months, which rewards centres that keep patients under review.

Two of the report's conclusions need restating for the American market. The first is the Wardley reading. We placed the antifibrotic class as a product drifting toward commodity, with value re-forming around diagnosis and reach. 340B means that in a large part of the US hospital sector, a commoditising medicine does not release value to be captured elsewhere — it withdraws the funding from the very activities we said would hold the value next.

The second is the list of enabling constraints "being set now, by default, mostly by people not in the room". There is a fourth, and it has a date: how the 340B spread is measured, routed and accounted for from 1 January 2027. We recommend that a REV.B of WC-006 carry it.

Part 07 / 07
§07

What follows

Three questions for a board. Five moves for a market access team. One position on reform.

7.1 · For the board

The line item is accurate. It is also incomplete.

01 / 03
What are we buying with it?

340B is reported as a deduction from gross sales. Ask for the other half of the entry: in our largest covered-entity accounts, which parts of our patients' pathway does that margin plausibly support, and how would we know?

02 / 03
What happens to our patients on 1 January 2027?

Not to our net price — to the clinic. Model the combined effect of negotiated prices, the outpatient payment cut and the rebate pilot on the ten centres that start the most patients on our medicines.

03 / 03
What is our position, and can we say it aloud?

"Reform" covers everything from transparency to abolition. A company whose patients depend on safety-net providers needs a position it could defend in front of those providers.

7.2 · For market access

Treat the spread as part of the pathway — then find out whether it is.

  1. Map dependence before the date, not after. For each priority centre: is it a covered entity, of which kind, does it dispense in-house or through contract pharmacy, and which unreimbursed roles sit closest to our patients? This is desk work plus a dozen conversations with pharmacy directors. It is cheap, and almost no one has done it.
  2. Separate the grantee from the hospital in every argument. The evidence supports different statements about each. A position that treats a federally qualified health centre and a $47-million-a-year hospital programme as the same thing is wrong about one of them.
  3. Ask for traceability, not abolition. The defensible ask is the one neither camp can easily refuse: that covered entities report net revenue and what it funds, and that the discount reach low-income patients at the counter. It would settle the argument in §03 with data. It may well show that the path is real.
  4. If the spread goes, ask what holds the path up. Where a rebate model or a negotiated price removes margin from a centre, the access pharmacist does not become unnecessary. Patient support, hub services and field reimbursement teams were designed around a provider with its own staff. Test whether they still fit.
  5. Run it as a set of probes. Three or four centres of different types, a declared question in each — what changed in time-to-first-fill, in abandonment, in persistence at six months — and a decision made in advance about what result would change our approach. The first quarter of 2027 is a natural experiment. It will run whether or not anyone is watching.
Our position on reform

The programme should be measured before it is cut, and it is being cut before it is measured.

We hold that both of the standard positions are too confident. The claim that 340B is a hospital subsidy with a charitable label cannot be shown, because the spending is unreported. The claim that every dollar reaches vulnerable patients cannot be shown for the same reason. What can be shown is that some of it builds care that nothing else pays for — and that is enough to make the manner of reform a clinical question as well as a financial one.

A cost to the product. A floor under the pathway.

The discount takes revenue from the medicine in every covered entity and gives the patient somewhere to stand in some of them. We cannot yet say in how many. The work worth doing between now and January is to find out — centre by centre, role by role — before four well-intentioned rules remove something nobody had written down.
